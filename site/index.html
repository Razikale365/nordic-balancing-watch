When Nordic balancing prices appear, and how often they change

The same electricity prices are published by several organisations, at different times, and are sometimes changed afterwards. Their websites only show the latest number. This site checks them every 15 minutes and keeps every version, so you can see who publishes first and what changed later.

What is an imbalance price?

Power companies promise in advance how much they will produce or use. Whoever ends up off that plan pays or is paid the imbalance price for each 15 minutes. It is only known after those 15 minutes are over.

Why does the timing matter?

Traders, battery operators and balance responsible parties act on this price. Knowing where it appears first, and how long it usually takes, tells them when they can rely on it.

Why do later changes matter?

If a published price is changed, anyone who acted on the first number acted on a different number from the final one. Analyses built on today’s final numbers quietly assume they were known sooner, and more accurately, than they were.

      Data & method

      How it is measured, and how to use the data

      First publication

      A price counts as published at the first check that returns it with a value. That is when the collector first saw it, not necessarily the moment the provider published it. Rows that appear without a price are not yet published.

      Imbalance prices are checked every 15 minutes and reserve capacity every hour, so a publication time is accurate to within one check.

      What is not counted

      Values already on record when collection started, or first seen after a collector outage, have no reliable publication time. They are left out of the delays and of the share changed.

      Statistics use a rolling 7-day window; the daily view covers closed UTC days.

      Revisions

      A revision is a change to a published price in euros, or a price that disappears and is still missing at the next check. Changes to other fields, such as demand or direction, are not revisions. The log keeps the earlier and the new price, and a 0.00 is shown as published.

      Energi Data Service recalculates its Danish-krone price from the euro price once a day. A change in the krone price alone is counted separately as a currency recalculation, not as a revision.

      eSett first publishes the Finnish price as 0.00 and replaces it with the settled price about an hour later. Once replaced, that 0.00 counts neither as publication nor as a revision. A 0.00 that Fingrid also shows is a real price and stays.

      Collected values are never edited or deleted. A value later found to be produced by a collection fault is marked and left out of the figures, and listed under corrections.

      Glossary

      Imbalance price
      The price paid or received, per megawatt-hour, by companies whose actual production or use differed from what they planned. Set separately for every 15 minutes and every price zone, after the fact.
      Settlement interval
      The 15-minute block that every imbalance price belongs to. Since 2023 the Nordic market settles in quarter-hours.
      Price zone (bidding zone)
      An area with one price. Denmark has DK1 (west) and DK2 (east); Finland is one zone (FI); Norway has NO1–NO5 (NO1 Oslo, NO2 south, NO3 middle, NO4 north, NO5 west); Sweden has SE1–SE4 from north to south.
      eSett
      The company that runs the joint imbalance settlement for Denmark, Finland, Norway and Sweden, owned by the national grid operators. Its figures are the ones the money is settled on.
      ENTSO-E
      The association of European grid operators. Its Transparency Platform republishes market data from every member country.
      TSO (national grid operator)
      Energinet in Denmark, Fingrid in Finland, Statnett in Norway and Svenska kraftnät in Sweden. Energinet and Fingrid publish imbalance prices on their own open-data sites.
      Balance responsible party
      A company that has taken on the financial responsibility for a group of producers or consumers being in balance. It pays or receives the imbalance price.
      Median
      The middle value: half of the intervals were faster, half slower. A good picture of a typical day.
      95th percentile
      95 out of 100 intervals were first seen by this time; only 1 in 20 took longer. A picture of a bad day.
      Revision
      Any change to a price after it was first published.
      Withdrawal
      A price that was published and later disappeared from the source.
      Reserve capacity
      Payment for being ready to raise or lower power at short notice. The grid operator buys it ahead of time and calls on it when needed.
      FCR-N and FCR-D
      Frequency containment reserves: the fastest reserves, reacting within seconds. FCR-N handles normal small swings, FCR-D (up and down) disturbances such as a power plant tripping.
      aFRR
      Automatic frequency restoration reserve: brings the frequency back to 50 Hz within minutes, on an automatic signal.
      mFRR
      Manual frequency restoration reserve: slower reserves activated on request, within about 15 minutes.
      D-2 and D-1 auctions
      Reserve capacity is bought two days (D-2) and one day (D-1) before delivery. A price published after the first auction can change when the next one clears.
